## Receipt vs invoice (use the right one)

A **receipt** confirms money already changed hands. An **invoice** requests payment that is still due. Mixing them confuses bookkeeping and customers. Use Receipt Maker for paid counter sales, booth sales, and immediate card-or-cash handoffs. Switch to the invoice tool when you are billing after delivery.

## What to put on every receipt

- Business name (and optional address or contact)
- Date and a unique receipt number
- Line items: description, quantity, unit price
- Subtotal, tax if you collect it, total
- Payment method note when useful (cash, card, transfer)
- Optional thank-you or return policy one-liner

## A booth-day workflow that survives chaos

1. Before the market opens, open [Receipt Maker](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/receipt-maker) and save a mental template: business name, tax line habits, typical item names.
2. For each sale, add complete lines—description, quantity, and price. Incomplete rows get flagged for a reason.
3. Preview, then download the PDF. Name it `receipt-2026-10-08-043.pdf` or similar.
4. Email or Airdrop the PDF if the customer wants digital; print if your booth has a small printer.
5. At night, log income and costs in [Expense Tracker](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/expense-tracker) so the receipt is not your only memory of the day.

If a sale is large enough that the buyer needs payment terms, stop and use [Invoice Generator](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/invoice-generator) instead of forcing a receipt shape onto an unpaid job.

## Honest limits

Receipt Maker creates a clear, professional record. It is not your accountant, not tax filing software, and not a certified fiscal device for jurisdictions that require special printers. Local rules for cash businesses vary. When in doubt, ask a qualified tax professional for your region. The tool’s job is a clean PDF you can hand over and archive—not legal advice.

## Naming and filing habits that prevent year-end panic

- One folder per month: `receipts/2026-10/`
- Receipt number sequence you control (do not restart at 001 every day unless you also encode the date)
- Keep the PDF even if you also emailed it
- Note refunds as separate dated files rather than editing history silently

Future-you will not remember which blue ceramic mug was receipt 17.

## Privacy and shared tablets

If you use a shared booth tablet, clear downloads when the day ends. Do not leave a customer’s email in an open tab. Browser tools that keep processing local still leave files in your Downloads folder—that is your responsibility.

## What “professional enough” means at a booth

You do not need foil letterhead. You need legible type, correct math, and a number you can find in December. Customers who expense craft supplies or job materials will thank you for a PDF they can forward to accounting without a photo of a napkin.

If you sell both physical goods and services the same day—say a mug plus a workshop seat—put them on separate lines with clear descriptions. Vague lines like “misc” create refund arguments later.

## Tax lines without overclaiming

If you collect sales tax, show it as its own line when your jurisdiction expects that presentation. If you do not collect tax, do not invent a tax line for looks. Receipt Maker is a formatting aid; your registration and rates come from local rules and, when needed, a tax professional.

## Refunds and partial returns

Issue a new dated document that references the original receipt number (“Refund re: receipt 2026-10-07-014”) rather than editing history in place. Keep both PDFs. Your future self settling a dispute needs the pair.

## Multi-staff booths

One person should own the receipt number sequence for the day, or encode staff initials in the number. Colliding “receipt 5” from two phones is how records dissolve. Agree before doors open.

## End-of-day close

Export or confirm every PDF landed in the month folder. Spot-check totals against cash drawer and card batch. When numbers disagree, the dated receipt list is your forensic trail—napkin math is not.
