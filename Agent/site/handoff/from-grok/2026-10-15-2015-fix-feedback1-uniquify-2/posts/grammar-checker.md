## Scope: outbound prose you own

Use this for proposal bodies, onboarding notes, and support replies you wrote. Do not paste an entire third-party contract and treat every flag as your error. Technical terms and brand slogans may look “wrong” while being intentional.

If the message also has a hard length cap, measure with [Word Counter](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/word-counter) after you accept edits so a clean sentence does not push you over a portal limit.

## Pass order that respects voice

1. Paste only the message body (exclude long quoted threads).
2. Fix clear grammar and article issues first.
3. Re-read proper nouns and dollar amounts by eye—tools miss invented product names.
4. Apply title rules with [Case Converter](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/case-converter) when a headline is included.
5. When two versions of a blurb must stay distinct, [Text Similarity Checker](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/text-similarity-checker) helps—but that is a different job from grammar.
6. Read the final paragraph aloud once before send.

## What to leave alone

Code samples, API paths, and legal defined terms often should not be “corrected.” British vs American spelling both work if you pick one locale and stick to it for the client. Product codenames that look like typos stay as the client wrote them in prior mail.

## Mistakes

Accepting every suggestion blindly. Proofing only inside the mail compose box with no offline copy. Skipping the human pass on numbers and names. Running marketing slogans through until they become bland. Grammar-checking a thread of quoted replies and “fixing” the client’s own sentences.

## FAQ

**Slogans?** Keep intentional fragments when brand voice needs them.

**Long PDFs?** Extract the section you wrote; do not treat a full scan as your prose.

**Order vs compare tools?** Grammar cleans sentences; comparing two full versions of a contract is a separate compare workflow.

**Does it replace an editor?** No—it catches common issues so your human review can focus on meaning.

## Tone is not a grammar error

Short sentences in a brand voice can look incomplete to a checker. Keep them when they are intentional. The goal is fewer accidental errors, not identical tone to a textbook. If every sentence becomes the same length, readers feel the rewrite even when every flag is “fixed.”

## Proposal-specific risks

Pricing tables in email bodies break across lines. After a grammar pass, re-check every figure against the attached PDF quote. Grammar tools will not notice a $4800 vs $4,800 mismatch that you introduced while editing.

Names of client stakeholders must be spelled as they spell them. Add them to a personal do-not-flag list mentally—verify manually. A single misspelled executive name undoes a clean grammar pass.

## Multilingual fragments

If a message includes a short phrase in another language, exclude it from the paste or accept that flags may appear. Do not “fix” a legal entity name into English. Do not translate a quote the client used for cultural reasons.

## Templates and signatures

Run grammar on the unique body, not on the entire signature block of legal disclaimers every time, unless you own those disclaimers. Repeated false positives train you to ignore real flags. Keep a clean template body and only paste the variable paragraphs.

## Feedback culture

When a client points out a typo that escaped, add that pattern to your pre-send list. Tools assist; accountability remains yours. Share the pattern with teammates so the same miss does not repeat on parallel proposals.

## Timing

Do the grammar pass when the content is otherwise frozen. Grammar-then-rewrite-then-forget-to-recheck is a classic miss. Block five minutes after “content lock” and before “send.”

When you use browser tools on deadline days, stay aware of 10 trial usages a day—paste the final body once rather than testing every sentence in isolation.

## Subject lines and preheaders

Many checkers never see the subject line if you only paste the body. Proof subject lines manually. Preheaders in marketing tools also need a human glance for truncated words. A perfect body with a broken subject still looks careless in the inbox list.

## Mini case: apology email

Apology emails attract scrutiny. Run Grammar Checker, then read aloud once. If a sentence sounds defensive after “fixes,” rewrite it in your voice. The tool catches grammar; you still own tone under pressure. Keep the apology short enough that Word Counter is not the main issue—clarity is.

## Mini case: onboarding note

A freelance designer sends a 180-word onboarding note with three checklist items. Grammar Checker flags one article error and one comma splice. After fixes, the checklist still uses short fragments on purpose. The client receives a note that sounds like the designer, not like a style manual.

## Numbers, units, and currency symbols

Grammar passes rarely validate whether $4,800 matches an attached quote. Build a tiny checklist beside the tool: amounts, dates, stakeholder names, product codes. Run Grammar Checker for language; run the checklist for facts. Mixing the two jobs into one skim is how wrong prices ship in clean English.

## Team handoff for freelancers

If a colleague drafts and you send, the grammar pass belongs to the sender of record. Do not assume the drafter already ran it. Paste the final body yourself. Ownership prevents “I thought you checked” gaps on client mail.

## When not to open the tool

If you are still inventing the offer, grammar is premature. Freeze content first. Early passes on unstable drafts create churn and false confidence. The tool shines after the argument is set and only language remains.
