## Spec sheets that ship to two markets

You manufacture in millimeters. A US retailer wants inches on the line sheet. Convert each critical dimension once, round to the precision they print, and show both units consistently.

Shipping teams need package cm to in before a carrier form that only accepts inches. That is a different number set—do not reuse product width as box width. Product and package are two rows, always.

## Example: 80 mm product width

80 mm → about 3.15 in at two decimals. Planogram slot 3.25 in. You note clearance instead of forcing a false “exact fit” claim. Height and depth get the same treatment on the same day so the sheet does not mix precisions.

Weight example: 1.25 kg → about 2.76 lb. Label both on the carton row if the carrier form wants pounds. Temperature example: storage 2–8 °C becomes a Fahrenheit range for a US warehouse poster—convert both ends of the range, not only the midpoint.

## Traps

Ounces vs fluid ounces. Temperature is not a linear “add thirty” trick under stress. Diameter confused with radius ruins packaging. Early rounding in multi-step calcs compounds error. Area is not converted like length. Volume conversions need the right category, not a length tool used twice by accident.

## Mistakes

Converting only one axis of a three-axis carton. Mixing unit systems on one row of a spreadsheet. Using random sample numbers from [Random Number Generator](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/random-number-generator) as if they were measurements (that tool is for sampling—not for specs). Overwriting the source millimeter column with inches and losing the factory truth.

## Limits

## Planogram and retail reality

Retail buyers live in inches and feet in some markets even when factories live in millimeters. Your line sheet should not force them to convert under fluorescent lights. Do the conversion once at the source of truth and freeze the sheet version.

## Manufacturing tolerances

If a part is 80.0 ± 0.2 mm, convert the nominal and state tolerance in the same unit system the buyer uses. Do not convert only the nominal and leave tolerance in mm on an inch sheet. Buyers who see mixed systems will assume the sheet is unreliable.

## Temperature for storage claims

Storage ranges in Celsius misread as Fahrenheit cause damaged goods claims. Convert storage guidance the same day you convert dimensions, and label units on every row. Cold-chain posters that omit the unit symbol are a lawsuit waiting for a busy warehouse.

## Team spreadsheet hygiene

One column for source unit, one for value, one for converted value, one for display string. Do not overwrite source measurements with converted ones. Protect the source columns or keep them on a hidden sheet with access control.

## Field work

On site with a tape in inches, write inches first, convert to mm back at the laptop for the factory. Reversing that order under time pressure causes transcription errors. Photograph the tape against the object when disputes are likely.

## Shipping dimensional weight

Carriers care about package outer dimensions. Product dimensions are not package dimensions. Measure the carton, convert if needed, then estimate cost. A product that is 300 mm deep does not make a 300 mm carton if the pack-out adds foam.

## Dual-unit invoices and customs

Customs forms may demand a specific unit. Convert for the form, keep factory units in your ERP, and never overwrite source fields. Mismatched commercial invoices delay shipments more often than people expect. Keep a short note of which form got which unit system.

## Mini case: shelf depth

Shelf depth 12 in vs product depth 300 mm. Convert 300 mm to inches first (about 11.81 in), then confirm clearance. Teams that compare 12 to 300 without converting create returns and angry buyers.

## Mini case: three SKUs, one buyer visit

A buyer visits tomorrow. Three SKUs still show only mm. Convert width, height, depth, and weight for all three in one session. Print dual-unit cards. The visit is not the time to discover a 0.1 in clearance miss on SKU two.

## Rounding rules buyers actually print

Agree whether you round to two decimals, nearest 1/16 inch, or nearest millimeter display. Convert full precision first, then round for the sheet. Rounding before conversion invents error. Write the rounding rule on the line sheet footer so factories and buyers share one policy.

## Weight for postage vs product net

Postage cares about package gross weight. Catalogs often show product net weight. Convert both if both appear, and label which is which. Mixing net and gross after unit conversion creates customs and carrier disputes that look like “math errors” but are category errors.

## Closing checklist for a dual-market launch

For each SKU: convert width, height, depth, weight, and storage temperature if claimed. Dual-label the sheet. Spot-check three SKUs with a physical tape against the converted inches. Ship the sheet only after the spot-check. Unit Converter is the arithmetic step; the tape is the reality check.
