## What BMR and macros mean here

BMR is the estimated energy your body uses at rest. Activity level scales that toward total daily needs. Macros allocate those calories across protein, carbohydrates, and fat for the goal you pick (maintain, lose, gain—as the tool presents them).

Activity is the input people overestimate most. "Somewhat active" when your week is desk + two walks will inflate the plan.

## Using the numbers without turning life into a spreadsheet cult

Treat outputs as planning ranges. Restaurant weeks, travel, and exams move the average. Prioritize protein target and overall calories before perfect fat/carb ratios unless a clinician told you otherwise.

## Common mistakes

- Entering "very active" because of one long hike last month.
- Cutting 1,000 calories overnight and calling it a plan.
- Ignoring protein while chasing low-carb trends that do not fit training.
- Using someone else's height/weight "to see what happens."
- Treating the tool as medical advice (it is not).

## Limits and good to know

- Estimates only—individual needs vary; clinicians and registered dietitians own medical nutrition.
- Honest activity beats optimistic activity every time.
- Not a food logger; pair with whatever tracking method you already tolerate.
- Non-file calculators are generally unlimited to use on the site's professional toolkit model.
- Goals and formulas can differ from other apps; pick one model for a month before switching.

## Grocery list habit

After you have macros, write a weekly list that covers protein anchors (eggs, yogurt, beans, chicken, tofu) before snacks. The calculator sets the target; the cart decides whether the week is possible.


## Two-week experiment design

Week 1: eat near the suggested calories with protein priority; weigh in 3 mornings after bathroom, average them. Week 2: keep protein, adjust calories ±200 only if the average trend clearly disagrees with the goal. Avoid daily panic changes from one salty meal.

Chris writes the weekly average in the same note as the calculator screenshot date so future-you knows which model produced the plan.

## Protein first shopping

Build the list around protein servings you will actually cook on exam weeks: Greek yogurt, eggs, frozen chicken, canned tuna, tofu, lentils. Carbs and fats fill around those anchors. A perfect macro app with empty fridge fails.

## Activity honesty examples

- Desk job + 2 gym days → light or moderate, not "athlete"
- Walking commute daily → can justify a bump, still not "very active" for most people
- Sport season weekends only → do not average the weekend into every weekday

## When to get human help

Rapid weight goals, medical conditions, disordered eating history, or sports with weight classes need a clinician or registered dietitian. The calculator is a planning aid, not a prescription.

## Pairing tools without overlap panic

Use BMR & Macro for the split, Calorie Calculator if you want another daily energy view, Water Intake for a simple hydration reminder. Pick primary numbers from one tool per month so you are not chasing three contradictory targets.

## FAQ-style notes

**Sex field?** Used by standard estimation formulas—enter as the tool asks; discuss clinical edge cases with a professional.
**Pregnant or medical diet?** Do not self-assign aggressive deficits; get supervised guidance.
**App disagrees?** Different formulas; stick to one for 30 days.


## Exam-week adjustments without abandoning the plan

On all-nighter weeks, keep protein targets and accept calorie variance from cafeteria food. Return to the written targets the Monday after exams rather than inventing a new extreme plan at 2 a.m. Consistency across months beats perfection across three days.


## Training day versus rest day optional split

If you train hard three days a week, some people prefer a slight calorie bump on training days and a slight cut on rest days that still averages the calculator total. Only do this after the base plan feels steady for two weeks—do not invent complex splits on day one.
