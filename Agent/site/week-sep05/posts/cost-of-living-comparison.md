## Why salary alone lies

A $15,000 bump sounds decisive until housing is $600 higher every month. Twelve months of that difference is $7,200 before utilities, parking, or childcare. Add a longer commute and you are trading money for hours you will not get back.

People often compare base salary only. They skip renter's insurance, state income tax differences, and the fact that a cheaper city may force car ownership. A good comparison forces those line items into the open where you can see them together.

## What to put in the tool

Start with housing, food, transportation, healthcare, and utilities. Those five usually explain most of the gap between cities. Then add childcare or school costs if they apply, and a realistic miscellaneous line for habits you will not drop after the move.

Use the [Unit Converter](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/unit-converter) if one listing is in square feet and another in square meters, or if you need to normalize commute distances. Small unit mistakes produce large monthly errors that feel trustworthy until you recheck.

When you have two totals, the [Percentage Calculator](https://www.freedailypro.com/tool/percentage-calculator) helps you express the gap as a percent of the new salary. A 12% cost increase against a 10% raise is not a raise. It is a lifestyle downgrade dressed as progress.

## A simple decision framework

Enter current city costs as you actually spend today, not aspirational budgets. Enter destination city costs with conservative rent and a realistic commute. Add the new salary after taxes if you can estimate them, or at least note tax differences clearly.

Look at leftover monthly cash, not only total expenses. Stress-test one bad case: rent two hundred dollars higher than the average, or one fewer transit option. If leftover cash rises and hours of life do not collapse, the move is probably a real raise.

If leftover cash is flat and stress is higher, walk away politely. There will be other offers. There will not be another chance to avoid a move that looked good only on LinkedIn.

## Common mistakes when comparing cities

Comparing downtown luxury rent to suburban averages. Ignoring parking. Forgetting that walkable often means higher rent per square foot. Treating one month of short-term rental research as permanent housing cost. Using national averages when you need a two-bedroom near a specific school.

Another trap is double-counting. If transportation already includes car ownership, do not paste a second huge auto line without checking. Keep categories clean so the tool's totals mean something when you revisit them a week later.

## When the tool is not enough

International moves need currency, visa, and healthcare system differences a U.S. metro dataset will not fully cover. High-earner tax situations need a real accountant. Dual-income households should model both jobs, not only the relocating partner's offer letter.

Still, a clear monthly comparison is the right first pass before you spend weekends touring apartments. It is faster than a spreadsheet you abandon, and private enough that offer details stay on your device while you work through scenarios.

## A worked micro-example

Suppose current take-home supports $2,400 rent and $450 transit in City A. City B offers +$1,200 per month gross. After a rough tax haircut that might be roughly $800 to $900 net. If City B rent is $3,100 and you need a car at $350 all-in, the raise is gone.

The comparison tool makes that arithmetic visible in one screen instead of a foggy gut feeling. If City B rent is $2,600 and transit still works, leftover cash improves and the move can be justified even without lifestyle cuts. Write both versions down before you talk yourself into optimism.

## Honesty about limitations

The tool does not know your family will watch the kids two days a week, or that you hate cooking and will eat out more in a new city. It does not price loneliness or career growth. Those are judgment calls. What it does well is stop you from celebrating a nominal raise that rent already spent.

## Practical checklist before you accept

Confirm housing with real listings, not only tool averages. Convert units so area and distance match. Express the cost gap as a percent of the new pay. Re-run the comparison with a pessimistic rent. Sleep on it once the numbers are written down.
